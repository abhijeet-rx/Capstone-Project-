Can We Detect an Air Compressor Failure
Before It Happens?

Air compressors are essential railway components. Unexpected breakdowns halt passenger service, incur severe maintenance costs, and reduce system reliability. Using the MetroPT-3 dataset, this research investigates how machine learning, anomaly detection, and an explainable hierarchical Health Index can identify abnormal compressor behaviour before failure occurs.

Problem Statement

Why Predictive Maintenance?

Traditional railway maintenance relies either on fixed calendar servicing or reacting after components seize mid-route. Predictive maintenance aims to identify subtle abnormal patterns early enough to support operational decisions before physical failure halts service.

The 4-Phase Failure Progression
PHASE 01 Normal

Stable mechanical and electrical operation within healthy quantiles.

PHASE 02 Abnormal

Subtle sensor deviations (oil warming, motor current spikes, pressure drift).

PHASE 03 Warning

Persistent anomaly across consecutive samples (P = 3) flags early warning.

PHASE 04 Failure

Critical air-leak breakdown; conventional alarms only trip here.

Core Objectives

Research Objectives

✓ Understand Drift: Analyse historical compressor telemetry to characterize healthy versus degrading behaviour.
✓ Model Benchmarking: Evaluate supervised, deep learning, and anomaly detection models for failure prediction.
✓ Prospective Generalisation: Test whether models fitted on earlier data generalise to later, unseen prospective failures.
✓ Construct Health Index: Design a transparent, state-conditioned 5-component diagnostic score.
✓ Suppress False Alarms: Apply temporal persistence filtering (P = 3) to eliminate transient isolated alerts.
✓ Explain Distribution Shifts: Use TreeSHAP and statistical hypothesis tests to map feature shifts between events.
Dataset Foundations

The Data Behind the Research

The MetroPT-3 dataset contains multivariate time-series telemetry collected from an Air Production Unit (APU) on an active passenger train. It covers February to September 2020 at a nominal 10-second sampling interval with four documented air-leak failure events.

Research Workflow

From Raw Sensor Data to Early-Warning Analysis

The investigation follows a strict 8-stage engineering process designed to simulate real-world railway predictive maintenance without future information leakage.

Stage 01 Data Preprocessing

Loaded time-series telemetry, resolved temporal gaps, verified sampling fidelity, and handled irregular intervals.

Stage 02 Failure Labelling

Defined prediction target as the 1-hour interval prior to failure onset. Active failure periods excluded from prospective evaluation.

Stage 03 Chronological Splitting

Partitioned data strictly by time (Train → Val → Test). Zero random shuffling to prevent future leakage.

Stage 04 Feature Engineering

Extracted 60-second rolling statistics (means, standard deviations, differences) conditioned on operating states.

Stage 05 Model Benchmarking

Trained and compared 12 models across Supervised ML, Tree Ensembles, Deep Learning, and Anomaly Detection.

Stage 06 Health Index Design

Combined 5 robust physical anomaly components into a unified, inspectable health score.

Stage 07 Persistence Filtering

Introduced a 3-observation (P = 3, ~30 s) filter to eliminate transient false alarms while preserving lead time.

Stage 08 Explainability & Shifts

Applied TreeSHAP and statistical hypothesis tests (Welch's t-test) to uncover sensor distribution changes between events.

Model Benchmarking

Comparing Different Machine-Learning Approaches

We evaluated 12 raw and sequence-based model configurations under chronological validation and held-out prospective testing. Because the test dataset is severely imbalanced with a positive-class prevalence of approximately 0.0611% (~0.000611), Average Precision (AP) is the primary metric:

Why Accuracy is Misleading in Predictive Maintenance: With only ~0.0611% positive failure observations, a trivial dummy classifier predicting every observation as "healthy" would achieve 99.94% accuracy while detecting exactly zero failures. Therefore, models must be evaluated using Average Precision (AP) relative to prevalence, ROC-AUC, and false-alarm burden.

Healthy-Period False-Alarm Burden by Model (Logarithmic Scale)

Comparing instantaneous thresholding (P = 1) vs 3-sample persistence filter (P = 3)

FIG 03 / LOG BURDEN
What this graph reflects: Compares the number of distinct false-alarm episodes generated during healthy test operations under instantaneous alerting (P = 1, red bars) versus a 3-consecutive-sample requirement (P = 3, teal bars) plotted on a logarithmic scale (10⁰ to 10⁴).
  • Order-of-Magnitude Suppression: At P = 1, isolated sensor spikes trigger thousands of false alarms. Requiring 3 consecutive abnormal observations (~30 s) reduces false-alarm episodes by orders of magnitude across virtually all architectures.
  • The Persistence Trade-Off: Persistence significantly improves alarm stability, but if set too high (P ≥ 10), it can delay alarms or completely miss valid failure events.

Distribution Shift Analysis

How Did Sensor Behaviour Change Between Failure Events?

To understand why supervised models struggled to generalise from Event 3 to Event 4, we compared pre-failure distributions during the 1-hour interval before failure onset using Welch's t-test and the Mann–Whitney U test:

The Core Contribution

Beyond Predicting Failure — Measuring Compressor Health

The proposed hierarchical Health Index combines 5 sensor-derived anomaly components into one continuous score. It calculates 60-second rolling windows conditioned on operating state changes (>30-second gaps define segment boundaries to prevent cross-state contamination), logarithmically scales against healthy q95,i references, and triggers an alert when HI(t) > Q99 = 0.644372 persists across P = 3 observations:

Mathematical Formulation
HI(t) = ⅕ ∑k=1..5 ln(1 + Fk(t) / Qtrain95,k)

Where Fk(t) is the component anomaly score, Qtrain95,k is its healthy calibration reference, and all 5 components are equally weighted.

Final Research Findings

What Did We Learn?

Insight 01

Generalisation is Difficult

Models that perform well on validation struggle when evaluated on a future failure event with different sensor dynamics.

Insight 02

Anomaly Monitoring Resists Overfitting

Tracking deviations from healthy operating norms complements and outperforms historical label-based failure prediction.

Insight 03

Persistence Suppresses Alarm Burden

Requiring anomalies to persist across 3 consecutive samples (~30 s) cuts false alarms by orders of magnitude.

Insight 04

Sensor Shift Explains Failure

SHAP and statistical hypothesis tests confirm that physical operating regimes shifted fundamentally between Event 3 and Event 4.

Scope & Boundaries What This Study Does Not Show

Single Held-Out Prospective Failure: Event 4 represents one documented prospective failure event in one physical system. It does not establish universal reliability across every compressor fleet or failure mode.
No Downtime or Cost Reduction Measured: While advance lead time of 15.84 hours was demonstrated, real-world reduction in maintenance repair hours, train delay fines, or component replacement cost was not experimentally measured.
SHAP is Attribution, Not Physical Causality: TreeSHAP scores quantify the mathematical reliance of a fitted ensemble model on specific input features; they do not establish thermodynamic or physical causation of mechanical failure.
Health Index is Not Remaining Useful Life (RUL): The score assesses progressive operational abnormality relative to healthy quantiles; it does not output a continuous statistical prognostic curve of hours until mechanical seizure.
Future External Validation (MetroPT-2): The proposed Health Index requires future external validation on MetroPT-2 and additional industrial equipment under real-time deployment constraints.
